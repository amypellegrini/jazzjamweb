Piano Block Chords

In this lesson we’ll learn different harmonisation techniques for playing block chords at the piano.

Block chords, also called locked hands, harmonise a melody so that every note gets its own chord, moving in parallel with the melody. The sound is closely associated with George Shearing, Red Garland and Oscar Peterson, and it's one of the quickest ways to make a melody sound like a full horn section at the piano.

The basic shape is simple: play the melody note on top with the right hand, fill in three chord tones directly beneath it in close position, and double the melody an octave lower with the left hand. Both hands move together as one block, which is where the name comes from.

If it's easier to think about, you can also build the same block from the bottom up: start with the root in the left hand, stack the three chord tones above it, and duplicate the melody an octave higher on top. The notes are the same; only the order you find them in changes.

Exercise Diatonic block chords in C

Play a block chord on every chord of the C major scale, one chord per beat: C6, Dm7, Em7, F6, G7, Am7, B°7 and back to C6. In each block the melody is the chord's root, doubled an octave lower in the left hand, so every chord sits in root position from the left hand up.

The goal is to get familiar with the block chord voicing of every chord the major scale gives you, so you can find each shape without thinking.

When the melody lands on a chord tone, that's all there is to it. The interesting question is what to do with the notes in between: the passing tones that don't belong to the chord. Each of the techniques we’ll look at next is a different answer to that question. All the examples are in C.

To build and practise every technique, we'll use Barry Harris's sixth-diminished scale, also known as the major bebop scale. In C, it's the C major scale with one extra note, G♯ (or A♭), between G and A: C, D, E, F, G, A♭, A, B. With eight notes instead of seven, the scale lines up with the beat: played in eighth notes, the four notes of C6 (C, E, G and A) land on the beat, and the four passing tones (D, F, A♭ and B) fall in between. The chord tones always stay on C6; each technique is a different way to harmonise the passing tones.

Diminished passing tones

Harmonise passing tones as diminished chords.

Between the notes of C6 (C–E–G–A) sit D, F, G♯ (or A♭) and B, and together those four notes spell a diminished seventh chord. For this explanation we'll use B°7.

So whenever the melody hits a passing tone, voice it as B°7 with the melody on top; when it returns to a chord tone, go back to C6. Played up the scale, the two chords alternate on every step, and the diminished chord pulls back into C6 the same way G7♭9 resolves to the tonic. This pairing of a sixth chord with its diminished neighbour is the heart of Barry Harris's sixth-diminished scale.

A diminished seventh is built from equal minor thirds, so the same four notes can be named as four different diminished chords: D°7, F°7, A♭°7 or B°7, depending on which note you treat as the root.

Coming down the scale works exactly the same way: C6 on the chord tones and B°7 on the passing tones.

Exercise Diminished passing tones, up and down

Play the C bebop scale up and back down in eighth notes, one block per note: C6 on the chord tones and B°7 on every passing tone.

The goal is to switch between the C6 and B°7 shapes without stopping, in both directions.

Diatonic passing tones

Harmonise passing tones as diatonic chords.

Instead of a diminished chord, you can stay inside the key and harmonise passing tones with another diatonic chord. The result is smoother and less dramatic than the diminished approach. The chord tones C, E, G and A are still harmonised as C6, each in its own position; only the passing tones change.

Rootless major ninth voicing

Drop the root from Cmaj9 and you're left with E–G–B–D.

That shape contains both D and B, so use it for those two passing tones: on D the block is voiced D–E–G–B–D, and on B it is voiced B–D–E–G–B, with the melody doubled in the left hand as usual. Because the rootless voicing shares E and G with C6, the passing chords barely move away from home.

Dominant seventh voicing

The fourth degree is the exception. In C that's F, which the rootless Cmaj9 shape doesn't contain, so it takes a plain dominant seventh instead: G7, voiced F–G–B–D–F, with F on top and doubled in the left hand. The two shapes are very close: both are stacked thirds sharing G, B and D. So as the melody climbs, the hands alternate between C6 on the chord tones and two similar shapes on the passing tones.

With that last piece in place, every note of the C major scale has a block chord: C6 on the chord tones, rootless Cmaj9 on D and B, and G7 on F. Keep the A♭ from the diminished technique as a passing tone between G and A: harmonised as B°7, it holds the shape of the scale in place, just like a bebop scale, so the chord tones keep landing on the beat.

Coming down the scale, every note keeps the voicing it has on the way up.

Exercise Diatonic passing tones, up and down

Play the C bebop scale up and back down in eighth notes, one block per note: C6 on the chord tones, the rootless Cmaj9 on D and B, G7 on F, and B°7 on A♭.

The goal is to find each of these voicings by hand, going up and coming down.

Chromatic passing tones

Harmonise the passing tone with the target chord's own voicing, shifted by a half step. This technique only works when approaching the target note chromatically.

Take the same bebop scale and treat A♭ and B as passing tones that approach their targets chromatically, always ascending. With A in the melody, the C6 voicing (C–E–G–A) can just as well be read as Am7 (C6 over A). A♭ leads to A, so it takes that voicing a half step lower: C♭–E♭–G♭–A♭, an A♭m7. B leads to C, so it takes the C6 voicing (E–G–A–C) a half step lower: D♯–F♯–G♯–B, a B6. Each approach block then slides up into place, A♭m7 into Am7 and B6 into C6.

Coming down the scale, the approaches change direction. B no longer leads to a note a half step away, so it falls back to the rootless Cmaj9 voicing, B–D–E–G–B. A♭ now approaches G from above, so it takes G's voicing a half step higher. G is voiced A–C–E–G, which you can read as C6 over its fifth or as Am7 over G; a half step up, that becomes B♭–D♭–F–A♭, a B♭m7 over A♭, which slides down into place. F works the same way: it now sits a half step above E, so it takes E's voicing a half step higher. E is voiced G–A–C–E, C6 over its third or Am7 over E; a half step up, that becomes A♭–B♭–D♭–F, a B♭m7 over F.

Exercise Chromatic passing tones, up and down

Play the C bebop scale up and back down in eighth notes, one block per note. Going up, A♭ and B approach A and C from a half step below, as A♭m7 and B6; coming down, A♭ and F approach G and E from a half step above, as B♭m7/A♭ and B♭m7/F, and B falls back to the rootless Cmaj9.

The goal is to feel how the approach chord changes with the direction of the melody.

2 5 1 passing tones

Harmonise passing tones with the chords of a major 2 5 1.

In C, those chords are Dm7, G7 and C6. The chord tones C, E and G stay on C6, the 1 chord. The second, fourth and seventh degrees of the scale, D, F and B, all belong to G7, the dominant built on the fifth degree, so each one is harmonised as G7 in the inversion that puts it on top: on D the block is D–F–G–B–D, on F it is F–G–B–D–F, and on B it is B–D–F–G–B. The sixth degree, A, is harmonised as Dm7, the 2 chord, inverted the same way: A–C–D–F–A.

Keep the A♭ between G and A as a diminished passing tone, harmonised as B°7 just like in Barry Harris's sixth-diminished scale, so the scale keeps its bebop shape and the chord tones land on the beat. Played up the scale, every passing tone becomes a small cadence: G7, B°7 and Dm7 lead back to C6 each time the melody lands on a chord tone.

Voicing the sixth degree as Dm7 has a particular effect on the way up: the last three notes, A, B and C, are harmonised as Dm7, G7 and C6, a complete 2 5 1 progression that carries the melody home to the top of the scale.

Coming down the scale, every note keeps the chord it has on the way up.

Exercise 2 5 1 passing tones, up and down

Play the C bebop scale up and back down in eighth notes, one block per note: C6 on C, E and G; G7 on D, F and B; B°7 on A♭; and Dm7 on A.

The goal is to hear every passing tone resolve back to C6, as a small 2 5 1, in both directions.
