Diminished passing tones

Harmonise passing tones as diminished chords.

The C major sixth-diminished scale is composed of chord tones matching C6 (C–E–G–A) and non-chord tones (D, F, G♯ or A♭ and B). The non-chord tones spell a diminished seventh chord. This chord can be considered a C major dominant, a rootless G7♭9 chord.

So whenever the melody hits a passing tone, voice it as G7♭9 with the melody on top; when it returns to a chord tone, go back to C6.

The two chords alternate on every step, and the diminished chord pulls back into C6 the same way G7♭9 resolves to the tonic. This pairing of a sixth chord with its diminished neighbour is the heart of Barry Harris's sixth-diminished scale.

A diminished seventh is built from equal minor thirds, so the same four notes can be named as four different diminished chords: D°7, F°7, A♭°7 or B°7, depending on which note you treat as the root. Note that depending on the chord root the notes may have different enharmonic spelling, even when in practice the notes at the piano are the same.

Exercise C major bebop scale with block chords, ascending and descending

Play the C bebop scale up and back down in eighth notes, one block chord per note: C6 on the chord tones and G7♭9 on every passing tone.

The goal is to switch between the C6 and G7♭9 shapes without stopping, in both directions.
