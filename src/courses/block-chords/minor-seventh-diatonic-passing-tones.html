Diatonic passing tones for the minor 7th

Harmonise the passing tones of the minor 7th as diatonic chords.

As with C6, you can leave the diminished chord aside and harmonise the passing tones with chords that stay inside the scale. The result is smoother and less dramatic than the diminished approach. The chord tones D, F, A and C are still harmonised as Dm7, each in its own position; only the passing tones change.

Rootless minor ninth voicing

You can use a rootless minor ninth voicing to harmonise the second degree of the scale.

Drop the root from Dm9 and you're left with F–A–C–E.

That shape contains E, so use it for that passing tone: on E the block is voiced E–F–A–C–E, with the melody doubled in the left hand as usual. Because the rootless voicing shares F, A and C with Dm7, the passing chord barely moves away from home.

Dominant seventh voicing

The fourth and sixth degrees of the scale, G and B♭, aren't in the rootless Dm9 shape. So they take a plain dominant seventh instead: C7, the dominant of F6. On G it is voiced G–B♭–C–E–G, and on B♭ it is voiced B♭–C–E–G–B♭, with the melody on top and doubled in the left hand. C7 sits one note away from the diminished chord of the previous lesson: E, G and B♭ stay where they are, and C takes the place of C♯.

With that last piece in place, every note of the scale has a block chord: Dm7 on the chord tones, rootless Dm9 on E, and C7 on G and B♭. Keep the C♯ from the diminished technique as a passing tone between C and D: harmonised as A7♭9, it holds the shape of the scale in place, so the chord tones keep landing on the beat.

Coming down the scale, every note keeps the voicing it has on the way up.

Exercise Diatonic passing tones for the minor 7th, up and down

Play the minor 7th bebop scale on D up and back down in eighth notes, one block per note: Dm7 on the chord tones, the rootless Dm9 on E, C7 on G and B♭, and A7♭9 on C♯.

The goal is to find each of these voicings by hand, going up and coming down.
