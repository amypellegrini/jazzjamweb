Chromatic passing tones

Harmonise the passing tone with the target chord's own voicing, shifted by a half step.

This technique only works when approaching the target note chromatically. Take the same bebop scale and treat G♯ (A♭) and B as passing tones that approach their targets chromatically, always ascending. With A in the melody, the C6 voicing (C–E–G–A) can just as well be read as Am7 (C6 over A). G♯ leads to A, so it takes that voicing a half step lower: B–D♯–F♯–G♯, a G♯m7. B leads to C, so it takes the C6 voicing (E–G–A–C) a half step lower: D♯–F♯–G♯–B, a B6. Each approach block then slides up into place, G♯m7 into Am7 and B6 into C6.

Coming down the scale, the approaches change direction. B no longer leads to a note a half step away, so it falls back to the rootless Cmaj9 voicing, B–D–E–G–B. A♭ now approaches G from above, so it takes G's voicing a half step higher. G is voiced A–C–E–G, which you can read as C6 over its fifth or as Am7 over G; a half step up, that becomes B♭–D♭–F–A♭, a B♭m7 over A♭, which slides down into place. F works the same way: it now sits a half step above E, so it takes E's voicing a half step higher. E is voiced G–A–C–E, C6 over its third or Am7 over E; a half step up, that becomes A♭–B♭–D♭–F, a B♭m7 over F.

Exercise Chromatic passing tones, up and down

Play the C bebop scale up and back down in eighth notes, one block per note. Going up, G♯ and B approach A and C from a half step below, as G♯m7 and B6; coming down, A♭ and F approach G and E from a half step above, as B♭m7/A♭ and B♭m7/F, and B falls back to the rootless Cmaj9.

The goal is to feel how the approach chord changes with the direction of the melody.
